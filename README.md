## Reading Approach

Rather than simply summarizing what I read, I hope to gradually develop four habits of thinking:

### 01. Position — Where Does This Work Stand?

Understand how a work builds upon, challenges, or extends existing literature.

If a paper finds that industrial policy increases innovation, have previous studies already established this relationship? Does this paper offer a new mechanism, stronger identification, or genuinely new evidence?


### 02. Evaluate — How Convincing Is the Evidence?

Examine the research design, identifying assumptions, and evidence supporting the author's conclusions.

As emphasized by [Angrist & Pischke (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.24.2.3), understanding **how an identification strategy works and why its assumptions are credible** is essential for evaluating causal claims.

Beyond p-values, [Imbens (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.35.3.157) highlights the importance of **effect sizes and statistical uncertainty**.

**Example:** A policy increases patent applications by an estimated 2 per firm (p = 0.03), with a 95% confidence interval of [0.2, 3.8]. Despite statistical significance, the effect's magnitude remains uncertain. Its economic importance also depends on patent quality, policy costs, and actual economic benefits.



### 03. Synthesize — How Does This Change My Understanding?

Connect findings and arguments across different studies rather than treating each paper in isolation.

 One study finds increased patent applications, while another finds little change in R&D spending. Are these findings contradictory, or do differences in measurement, context, or mechanisms explain them?

### 04. Extend — What Questions Follow?

Identify unresolved questions, alternative explanations, and opportunities for further inquiry.

If a policy increases patenting activity, does it encourage genuine technological progress or merely change patenting incentives? What additional evidence could distinguish these explanations?

---

These are guiding principles, not a fixed reading protocol. Not every note needs to address all four dimensions.

The aim is not to produce exhaustive summaries, but to develop better judgment, intellectual curiosity, and independent thinking through reading.
